Consent Management for the DPDP Act: Notices, Records and Withdrawal

Consent under DPDP must be free, specific, informed and as easy to withdraw as to give. We help you design the journey and the records behind it.

Last reviewed: 8 November 2026 by the PrivaGlobe privacy team

Schedule a consultationRequest a DPDP assessment
Quick answer

DPDP consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, given through a clear affirmative action, and a Data Principal must be able to withdraw it as easily as it was given. Consent management is the process and tooling that captures, records and honours those choices.

What a DPDP-ready consent setup includes

  • Notice (Rule 3): plain language, itemised data and purposes, and links to withdraw and complain.
  • Capture: separate choices for separate purposes, with no pre-ticked boxes.
  • Record: who agreed, to what, when and which notice version.
  • Withdrawal: a simple route that stops processing and flows to vendors.
  • Children (Rule 10): verifiable parental consent where the user is a child.
  • Consent managers: the registration framework starts on 13 November 2026, and we help you decide whether to work with one.

Platform or process?

Some organisations need a consent platform. Others get most of the value from better notices and a clear record. We start with the process and recommend tooling only where it earns its place. See our privacy software and the guide to consent management under the DPDP Act.

This page is general information, not legal advice. Laws and official dates change, so we confirm the current position for your situation at the start of every engagement.

Frequently asked questions

Do we need a consent management platform?

Not always. Smaller organisations can meet the duties with clear notices and a reliable record. Larger ones with many channels usually benefit from tooling.

What about existing customers?

Their data was collected under earlier terms. We help you plan how to give notice and refresh consent where the law requires.

Is consent the only legal ground?

No. The Act also allows certain legitimate uses, such as for employment or legal obligations. We map each purpose to the right ground.
