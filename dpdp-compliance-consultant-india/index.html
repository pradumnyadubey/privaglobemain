What is the DPDP Act, and why does it matter now?

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act) is India's first dedicated data protection law. It governs how organisations collect, use, store and share the digital personal data of individuals, and it gives those individuals enforceable rights. The Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025 were notified in November 2025 and bring the Act into operation in phases. Based on the published timeline, the consent manager framework begins around November 2026 and the main obligations, along with penalties, apply from roughly mid-May 2027. Dates can be refined by the government, so we confirm the current position for you at the start of every engagement.

Eighteen months sounds long, but a realistic DPDP programme involves finding where personal data lives, rewriting notices, rebuilding consent flows, renegotiating vendor contracts and training staff. Most organisations that start early finish calmly. Those that wait end up doing the same work under pressure.

Does the DPDP Act apply to your business?

The Act applies to the processing of digital personal data inside India, whether it was collected online or collected offline and later digitised. It also applies outside India where you process data in connection with offering goods or services to people in India. In practice that covers Indian companies of every size, and foreign companies that sell to Indian customers.

The Act uses its own vocabulary. A Data Fiduciary decides why and how personal data is processed (broadly, the "controller"). A Data Processor processes data on a fiduciary's behalf. A Data Principal is the individual the data is about. The government can designate certain fiduciaries as Significant Data Fiduciaries based on factors such as the volume and sensitivity of data, and these face extra duties. Our applicability assessment tells you which role you hold for each processing activity.

What the DPDP Act requires from a Data Fiduciary

  • Notice: a clear, itemised notice telling people what data you collect, why, how to exercise their rights and how to complain, available in English or any language listed in the Eighth Schedule to the Constitution.
  • Consent: free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, given through a clear affirmative action and as easy to withdraw as to give. The Act also recognises a limited set of "legitimate uses" where consent is not needed.
  • Purpose limitation and retention: use data only for the purpose notified, and erase it once the purpose is served unless a law requires you to keep it.
  • Security safeguards: reasonable technical and organisational measures to prevent a personal data breach.
  • Breach intimation: inform the Data Protection Board of India and affected individuals when a breach occurs, with a detailed report to the Board within the timeline set by the Rules.
  • Children's data: verifiable consent of a parent or guardian before processing the data of anyone under 18, and no tracking, behavioural monitoring or targeted advertising directed at children, subject to the exemptions in the Rules.
  • Data Principal rights: access to information, correction and erasure, grievance redressal, and the right to nominate someone to exercise rights on their behalf.
  • Processor oversight: you remain responsible for personal data handled by processors, so contracts and vendor due diligence matter.

Significant Data Fiduciaries must additionally appoint a Data Protection Officer based in India, engage an independent data auditor and carry out periodic data protection impact assessments. If this may apply to you, see our DPO as a Service page.

Penalties under the DPDP Act

The Data Protection Board of India can impose monetary penalties. The Act's schedule sets the ceilings.

Breach of obligationMaximum penalty
Failure to take reasonable security safeguards to prevent a personal data breachUp to ₹250 crore
Failure to notify the Board and affected individuals of a breachUp to ₹200 crore
Breach of obligations relating to children's dataUp to ₹200 crore
Breach of additional obligations of a Significant Data FiduciaryUp to ₹150 crore
Any other breach of the Act or RulesUp to ₹50 crore

Penalties are ceilings, not fixed amounts. The Board considers the nature, gravity and duration of the breach, the type of data and the steps taken to mitigate harm, which is why documented, demonstrable controls matter as much as the controls themselves.

Frequently asked questions

Does the DPDP Act apply to small businesses and start-ups?

Yes, in principle. The Act applies by activity, not company size, so any organisation processing digital personal data of individuals is covered. The Rules provide limited exemptions for certain categories, which we assess case by case. Smaller organisations usually need a leaner programme, not none.

What is the DPDP compliance deadline?

The Rules, 2025 phase in obligations. The consent manager framework starts around November 2026 and the main obligations and penalties apply from roughly mid-May 2027. We confirm the latest official dates when you engage us, because the government can amend timelines.

Is a Data Protection Officer mandatory under DPDP?

A DPO based in India is mandatory only for Significant Data Fiduciaries. Other fiduciaries must publish contact details of a person who can answer questions about processing, and many appoint a DPO voluntarily as good governance.

How is DPDP different from GDPR?

Both are rights-based, but DPDP is built around consent and a short list of legitimate uses, while GDPR offers six lawful bases. DPDP has no separate category of sensitive personal data, and it treats all under-18s as children. See our GDPR consulting page if you also serve EU customers.

What does a DPDP compliance engagement include?

It typically starts with an assessment and gap report, followed by documentation, implementation support and an independent readiness review. We scope each engagement to your size, systems and risk, and you can buy individual phases.

Can you support us if we also need GDPR or CCPA compliance?

Yes. We map overlapping obligations once and apply a single control framework, adding local steps where each law requires them.
