Data Privacy Glossary: DPDP, GDPR and CCPA Terms Explained

Short, plain definitions of the terms you will meet in India's DPDP Act, the EU's GDPR, California's CCPA and other privacy laws.

Last reviewed: 28 October 2026 by the PrivaGlobe privacy team

Personal data

Any information about an identifiable individual, such as a name, phone number, email address, ID number, location or online identifier. Most privacy laws apply only to data that relates to a living person who can be identified directly or indirectly.

Data Fiduciary

The DPDP Act term for an organisation that decides why and how personal data is processed. It is roughly equivalent to a controller under the GDPR and carries the main compliance duties.

Data Principal

The DPDP Act term for the individual whom personal data relates to. For children and persons with disabilities, the parent or lawful guardian acts as the Data Principal.

Significant Data Fiduciary

A Data Fiduciary designated by the Indian government because of factors such as the volume and sensitivity of data it handles. It must appoint an India-based Data Protection Officer, engage an independent data auditor and run periodic impact assessments.

Consent manager

Under the DPDP Rules, a registered entity that gives individuals a single, accessible way to give, manage, review and withdraw consent. The framework starts on 13 November 2026.

Data controller

The GDPR term for the organisation that decides the purposes and means of processing personal data. It bears the primary responsibility for compliance.

Data processor

An organisation that processes personal data on behalf of a controller or Data Fiduciary, for example a cloud provider or payroll vendor. Processors work under a written contract and the controller stays accountable.

Data subject

The GDPR term for the individual whom personal data relates to. It is the counterpart of the DPDP Act's Data Principal.

Data Protection Officer (DPO)

The person who oversees data protection compliance, advises management, and acts as the contact point for regulators and individuals. Whether one is mandatory depends on the law and your activities. See our DPO as a Service page.

Data protection impact assessment (DPIA)

A structured assessment of the risks a processing activity poses to individuals and the measures to reduce them. The GDPR requires one for high-risk processing, and the DPDP Act requires periodic ones for Significant Data Fiduciaries.

Lawful basis

The legal ground that justifies processing personal data. The GDPR lists six: consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task and legitimate interests.

Legitimate interests

A GDPR lawful basis that allows processing needed for the controller's or a third party's interests, unless those interests are overridden by the individual's rights. It requires a documented balancing test. The DPDP Act has no equivalent general basis.

Legitimate use

A DPDP Act ground, listed in the Act, that allows processing without consent in defined situations such as a legal obligation, a medical emergency or certain employment purposes.

Special category data

GDPR term for sensitive data such as health, biometric, genetic, racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religion and sexual orientation. Processing it needs an additional legal condition.

Valid consent

Consent that is freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous, expressed through a clear affirmative action and as easy to withdraw as to give. Pre-ticked boxes and bundled consent do not meet this standard.

Records of processing (RoPA)

A written record of what personal data an organisation processes, why, where it goes and how long it is kept. The GDPR requires one for many organisations, and it is the foundation of any privacy programme.

Data subject access request (DSAR)

A request from an individual to see, correct, delete or move their personal data. Laws set deadlines for responding, for example one month under the GDPR.

Personal data breach

A security incident that leads to the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, disclosure of or access to personal data. Many laws require notifying the regulator and sometimes affected individuals.

Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs)

Pre-approved contract terms that allow personal data to move from the EU to a country without an adequacy decision, usually alongside a transfer impact assessment.

Adequacy decision

A formal finding by the European Commission that a country offers an essentially equivalent level of data protection, so data can flow there without extra safeguards. India does not currently have one.

Cross-border data transfer

Moving or giving access to personal data in another country. Laws regulate it in different ways, from transfer safeguards under the GDPR to government-restricted countries under the DPDP Act.

Privacy by design

Building privacy protections into systems, products and processes from the start, rather than adding them later. The GDPR makes it a legal duty.

Data minimisation

Collecting and keeping only the personal data that is necessary for a stated purpose, and no more.

Purpose limitation

Using personal data only for the purposes given to the individual when it was collected, unless the law allows something else.

Retention schedule

A document that sets how long each category of personal data is kept and when it is deleted or anonymised.

Pseudonymisation

Replacing identifying details with a code so data cannot be linked to a person without extra information kept separately. It reduces risk but the data is still personal data.

Profiling

Automated use of personal data to evaluate or predict aspects of a person, such as behaviour, preferences or location. The GDPR gives people rights around profiling and automated decisions.

Do Not Sell or Share

A CCPA/CPRA right to tell a business not to sell or share personal information for cross-context advertising. Businesses must offer a clear opt-out link and honour browser opt-out signals such as Global Privacy Control.

Terms differ slightly between laws. Where a DPDP term and a GDPR term mean nearly the same thing, we say so. For a full comparison see DPDP vs GDPR.
